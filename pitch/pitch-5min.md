## 1 · Promise (0:10)
> Good morning. We're main.dart, and this is PACE. Our promise in one line: PACE helps students who keep running late start the right thing at the right time.

**Signpost:** "Let us show you what that looks like on a real morning."

## 2 · Problem hook (0:30)
> Meet Baby. 7:41 AM. Class starts at 8:00. She just woke up. *(pause)*
> She skips breakfast, runs, and walks in at 8:15. The quiz has started. And the lab report due at 9? She only remembered it at 11 last night.
> Baby is made up, but this morning isn't: it's ours. And about three in four college students call themselves procrastinators.

*Guide check:* a **person** (a 2nd-year student), a **moment** (the 40 minutes before class), a **consequence** you can check (marked late, 15 minutes of a quiz lost). The slide labels the story "illustrative".

**Signpost:** "We've named the problem. Here's the workflow we propose."

## 3 · Solution: the workflow change (0:35)
> Today, wake time is a guess, you leave when you notice the clock, deadlines show up the night before, and one bad day resets your streak.
> With PACE, you enter your class time once, and it works out when to wake, eat and leave, with a reminder at each step. If you're late, it shows one thing: "Leave now." Deadlines get four reminders that come closer. And on a bad day, low-energy mode shows only what's close. No streaks, no red.
> That's our key difference: PACE does the planning, and it forgives.

**Signpost:** "And this isn't a mock-up. Here's the app."

## 4 · Built (0:20)
> This is the real Today screen. Open the app and you see "Right now": the next step and its time. Late? It switches to "Leave now. You arrive about 08:05."
> It's built and tested: 335 automated tests, 13 modules, no account, and your data stays on your phone. We're still checking reminder timing on real phones.

**Signpost:** "So who is this for?"

## 5 · Market (0:20)
> Our first buyers are college students on Android who start the day behind. Worldwide that's about 147 million people, an estimate built from UNESCO and StatCounter figures.
> But the segment we start with is our classmates: our 30 classmates, over 8 weeks.

**Signpost:** "Here's how PACE makes money."

## 6 · Business model (0:25)
> Who pays? The student, or a parent. How often? ₱49 a month, or ₱399 a year. Why? So you never lose your plan: sync and backup across your phone and laptop.
> The planner itself stays free forever, because the students who need it most shouldn't hit a paywall. Tiimo, a similar app, does the same; about one in ten of its users pay. Our price is an assumption, and we'll test it.

**Signpost:** "Before any forecast, here's how we'll prove it."

## 7 · Validation (0:35)
> We don't have customer data yet, so here's exactly how we'll get it. Three tests, and each one could prove us wrong. *(pause)*
> Problem proof, week 1: survey all 30 classmates and interview 8. Are they late to first class at least once a week? That number is our baseline.
> Use proof, week 2: can 10 classmates set up their week alone in under five minutes?
> Business proof, weeks 3 to 10: a pilot with all 30 classmates. Are they still using it in week 8? Are they late less often than at baseline? Will they pre-order Plus?

**Signpost:** "If it works, is ₱49 worth it to a student? Here's our model."

## 8 · ROI (0:25)
> Is ₱49 worth it? Here's an honest model, and every number on this slide is an assumption.
> If PACE saves a student five minutes a school day of deciding and scrambling, that's 110 minutes a month. Valued at minimum wage, about ₱94 an hour, that's ₱172. Minus the ₱49 fee, that's ₱123 a month. *(pause)*
> Even more simply: Plus pays for itself if PACE saves just 1.4 minutes a day. We'll measure the real number in the pilot. We left out grades and stress because we can't measure them fairly.

**Signpost:** "So how do the first students find us?"

## 9 · Go-to-market (0:20)
> Two steps. First, our own classroom: 30 classmates, on their own phones; that's the pilot.
> After the pilot, we go straight to a public Play Store listing, with short videos featuring Tuck so students at other schools can find it.

**Signpost:** "What do students use today instead?"

## 10 · Alternatives (0:20)
> Phone alarms ring at a time you guessed. Calendars and to-do apps store what's due, not when to start. Visual planners like Tiimo look calm, but you fill them in yourself.
> PACE is the one that works out the morning for you and turns lateness into one next step.

**Signpost:** "Here's the team building it."

## 11 · Team (0:15)
> We're main.dart: seven of us. Together we bring app development, design and testing; that's how PACE got built. Our gaps are user research and marketing, and the pilot is how we close them.

**Signpost:** "Which brings us to our ask."

## 12 · Ask (0:25)
> Our ask: ₱4,500 to run an 8-week pilot with our 30 classmates. *(pause)*
> We don't need to buy devices: our own and our classmates' phones already cover several brands. So the money thanks each classmate with ₱50 of load for eight weeks of surveys, runs one onboarding session, and pays the one-time Play Store fee.
> By week 10 we'll report: 30 classmates onboarded, how many still use it, whether they're late less often, and how many would pay.
> So the decision we're asking for: approve and fund the pilot in our classroom.

*Stop. Then next slide.*

## 13 · Close (0:05)
> Thank you. We'd love your questions.

*Stop talking and wait. Demo only if asked or if there's time: Today → tap a step → Deadlines → Just start → low-energy mode.*

## Red flags
- **No customer evidence yet.** The validation slide says "planned, no results yet". Keep that label until you have data; if you run the week-1 survey before the pitch, replace the plan with real numbers (sample size, period, baseline, metric).
- **Reminders not verified on a real phone.** Don't promise reliability.
- **The ROI is a model.** Say "assumption" out loud; the guide's own example does.

## Yellow flags
- ₱49 / ₱399 pricing is untested; Plus needs accounts (M11), which aren't built yet.
- The NCR minimum wage figure (₱755/day) has conflicting wage-order references in news sources; verify on the DOLE/RTWPB site.
- Budget lines are estimates; check the peso rate before the pitch.
- The pilot is one classroom (30 people who know us): a first signal, not a representative sample. Say so if asked.
- The alternatives table is our reading of public product pages.
